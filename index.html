6 Monitor performance over time

AI performance can drift as patients, practice, and models change, so regulators — including the FDA in its expectations for AI-enabled devices — look for ongoing monitoring, not a one-time validation. This dashboard replays your reviewed cases period by period, as if you were re-checking the tool on a schedule.

Side by side: each period's ROC curve

The orange square is your locked cut-off applied to that period's cases; the hollow circle is the cut-off that would have been best for that period alone. When they drift apart, the locked rule no longer serves your priorities.

How this tool works

    
    
      
  1. The AI never decides on its own. For every past patient in your file, the tool
        asks the AI one neutral question: “what is the probability that …?”. It never asks the AI
        to make the decision.
    2. 
      
  2. The curve shows every possible cut-off. If you act whenever the AI's probability
        is above some cut-off, you catch more true cases (good) but also act unnecessarily more often
        (costly). The curve shows this trade-off for every possible cut-off, computed against your
        gold-standard outcomes.
    3. 
      
  3. You choose the trade-off, not the AI. You state how bad a missed case is
        compared with an unnecessary action (for example “missing a real emergency is 10× worse than one
        unnecessary referral”). The tool then finds the cut-off on the curve that best serves that
        priority — even if the AI's probabilities are systematically too high or too low, because the
        cut-off is chosen from your own data.
    4. 
      
  4. New cases are decided with the same recipe. Ask the AI only for a probability,
        then apply your chosen cut-off. This keeps decisions consistent, auditable, and under the
        hospital's control.
    5. 
      
  5. Performance is monitored, not assumed. If your file includes case dates, the
        tool replays the review period by period — side-by-side ROC curves, AUROC over time, and mistake
        rates at your locked cut-off — so drift is caught early and the cut-off can be re-estimated.
        Ongoing monitoring of this kind is part of what regulators such as the FDA look for in
        AI-enabled clinical tools.
    6. 
    

    
Method: Yamin et al., “Talk is not cheap: Eliciting beliefs and utilities is
      necessary for trustworthy medical AI decisions.” The analysis here is the same as the

Important: this tool supports — it does not replace — clinical
      judgment. Validate on your own data, re-check periodically, and follow your institution's
      governance process before using AI-assisted decisions in care.
